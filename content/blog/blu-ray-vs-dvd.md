**Blu-ray is much sharper than DVD: a Blu-ray stores 1080p video (1920×1080), while a DVD is standard definition at 720×480 in the US, so Blu-ray carries six times as many pixels per frame.** It also has about four times the video bitrate and can hold lossless audio. On any modern HD or 4K TV the difference is easy to see, but not every DVD on your shelf is worth replacing, because the gain depends on the film's source.

This guide covers what changes between the two formats, what you actually see on a real TV, and a quick way to decide which DVDs to upgrade and which to keep.

:::stats
- 6× | the pixels per frame | 1920×1080 vs a US DVD's 720×480
- 4× | the maximum video bitrate | 40 Mbit/s vs 9.8 Mbit/s
- 5× | the storage on one layer | 25 GB vs 4.7 GB
:::

<div class="tldr">

**TL;DR**

- **Blu-ray wins on every spec:** 1080p vs 480p, roughly 4× the bitrate, and lossless audio instead of compressed Dolby Digital.
- **You'll see it on any HD TV,** and it gets more obvious as screens get bigger.
- **Upgrade first:** films you rewatch, anything shot on 35mm or 70mm with a new HD master, animation, and non-anamorphic DVDs.
- **Keep the DVD:** for shows made in standard definition, cuts or extras that never reached Blu-ray, and films you rarely watch.

</div>

## What is the difference between Blu-ray and DVD?

Both are 12 cm discs read by a laser, but Blu-ray is a newer high-definition format. DVD-Video reached the US in March 1997; Blu-ray followed in June 2006. Blu-ray's 405 nm blue-violet laser reads much smaller pits than DVD's 650 nm red laser, so the same size disc holds far more data. Here's the side-by-side, from the [DVD-Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD-Video) and [Blu-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blu-ray) format references:

| | DVD | Blu-ray |
|---|---|---|
| Resolution | 720×480 (NTSC) or 720×576 (PAL) | 1920×1080 |
| Common name | 480p / 576p, standard definition | 1080p, full HD |
| Max video bitrate | 9.8 Mbit/s | 40 Mbit/s |
| Video codecs | MPEG-2 | MPEG-2, AVC (H.264) or VC-1 |
| Capacity | 4.7 GB single layer, 8.5 GB dual layer | 25 GB single layer, 50 GB dual layer |
| Best audio | Dolby Digital up to 448 kbit/s, DTS up to ~1.5 Mbit/s | Dolby TrueHD, DTS-HD Master Audio, uncompressed PCM |
| Laser | 650 nm red | 405 nm blue-violet |
| Region coding | Regions 1–6 (plus 0/all) | Regions A, B and C |
| Plays in | DVD, Blu-ray and 4K UHD players | Blu-ray and 4K UHD players only |

If you're also weighing 4K, that's a separate step up with HDR and wider color, covered in our [4K Blu-ray vs Blu-ray guide](/blog/4k-blu-ray-vs-blu-ray/).

## Is Blu-ray quality really that much better than DVD?

Resolution gets the headlines, but three things work together.

:::bars Pixels per frame (thousands)
- DVD, NTSC (US) | 346 | 345,600
- DVD, PAL (UK/Europe) | 415 | 414,720
- Blu-ray | 2074 | 2,073,600
:::

:::cards
### Detail
Six times the pixels means you can read text on signs, see fabric texture and pick out faces in a wide shot. On a DVD those details blur together.
### Compression
Blu-ray has about four times the bitrate and a more efficient codec. Dark scenes, smoke, rain and film grain are where DVDs fall apart into blocks and smearing.
### Sound
DVD audio is lossy Dolby Digital or DTS. Most Blu-rays carry a lossless track, which is bit-for-bit the studio master. You'll notice it most on a surround system.
### Widescreen
Every Blu-ray is natively 16:9. Many early DVDs were letterboxed inside a 4:3 frame, which wastes even more of their already limited resolution.
:::

So yes, the gap is real. But "how much better" depends on the film more than the format.

:::pullquote
The format sets the ceiling. The master decides whether a disc gets anywhere near it.
:::

## Does a DVD look bad on an HD or 4K TV?

A modern TV has to stretch a DVD's 345,600 pixels across a screen with 2 million (HD) or 8 million (4K). A Blu-ray or 4K player upscales the DVD first, which smooths jagged edges, but upscaling can't invent detail that was never on the disc.

:::versus Upscaled DVD | Blu-ray
- Clean and watchable on a 40-inch TV from the couch
- Noticeably soft on 55 inches and up
- Blocky in dark scenes, smoke and grain
- Compressed Dolby Digital or DTS audio
---
- Sharp from any normal seating distance
- Fine detail holds up on big screens and projectors
- Grain looks like film, not noise
- Usually lossless TrueHD or DTS-HD MA audio
:::

:::warning Watch for non-anamorphic DVDs
Some early widescreen DVDs aren't "enhanced for 16:9 TVs." On a modern TV they appear as a small picture with black bars on all four sides, and zooming in makes them softer still. If the back of the case doesn't say *anamorphic* or *enhanced for widescreen TVs*, that disc belongs at the top of your upgrade list.
:::

## Which DVDs are worth upgrading to Blu-ray?

Collectors on forums like DVD Talk and r/dvdcollection tend to land on the same pattern: the bigger the gap between the original source and DVD, the bigger the payoff. Old films shot on 35mm often look transformed by a new HD scan, while material that only ever existed in standard definition barely changes.

| Upgrade first | Usually fine to keep on DVD |
|---|---|
| Films shot on 35mm or 70mm with a new HD or 4K scan | Shows shot and finished on standard-definition video |
| Classic films restored for Blu-ray, like many Criterion and Warner releases | Films you watch once every few years |
| Animation, especially hand-drawn and CG features | DVDs with cuts or extras the Blu-ray left out |
| Effects-heavy blockbusters and anything with a big surround mix | Early, budget Blu-rays made from the same old master |
| Non-anamorphic or letterboxed DVDs | Concert videos and documentaries shot on SD video |

A classic example of a DVD worth keeping: the 2006 limited-edition DVDs of the original *Star Wars* trilogy include the unaltered theatrical cuts as bonus discs. They're non-anamorphic and look dated, but those versions have never had an official Blu-ray release.

## How do you decide what to upgrade without overspending?

Replacing a whole DVD shelf at once is expensive and mostly unnecessary. Work through it in this order instead:

:::steps
### List what you own on DVD
You can't prioritize a pile. Catalog the collection with the format on every entry, so you can filter to DVDs only and see the full list in one place. Our [cataloging guide](/blog/how-to-catalog-dvd-blu-ray-collection/) walks through it.
### Flag your rewatches
Mark the films you watch at least once a year. These are where the upgrade pays off most, because you'll see the improvement every time.
### Check the master, not just the format
Read a review of the Blu-ray before you buy. A new scan from the original negative is a big jump; a reused DVD-era master is not.
### Note what the DVD has that the Blu-ray doesn't
Commentaries, documentaries and alternate cuts sometimes get dropped. Write it down before you sell or donate the old disc.
### Upgrade a few at a time
Buy the top five, watch them, and decide whether the difference justifies the next five. Set a wishlist for specific editions so you buy the right one.
:::

{{phone:collection|Filter to DVD, see your upgrade list|In Movieshelf every title records its format, so search and filters can show just your DVDs. Add the Blu-ray editions you want to your watchlist and check them in the store before you buy.}}

:::tip Keep both for a while
If you buy the Blu-ray, don't get rid of the DVD the same day. Compare a scene you know well on both. Some older Blu-rays use heavy noise reduction that makes faces look waxy, and you might prefer the DVD's grain.
:::

## Can a Blu-ray player play DVDs, and vice versa?

Almost every Blu-ray player, 4K UHD player and Blu-ray-equipped game console plays DVDs and upscales them, so you don't need two machines. It doesn't work the other way: a DVD player can't read a Blu-ray at all, because its red laser can't resolve Blu-ray's much smaller data.

Region codes are the catch. DVDs use regions 1 to 6 and Blu-rays use A, B and C, and a player only plays its own region unless it's region-free. If you collect imports, see our explainers on [DVD region codes](/blog/dvd-region-codes/) and [which Blu-ray region the US uses](/blog/what-blu-ray-region-is-the-us/).

:::checklist Before you replace a DVD
- The film is one you'll actually rewatch
- The Blu-ray uses a new or well-reviewed master
- You've noted any DVD-only extras or cuts
- The Blu-ray's region matches your player
- You've checked your catalog so you don't buy it twice
:::

## Is DVD still worth buying in 2026?

Sometimes. DVDs are often the cheapest way to own a film, especially used, and some titles, TV seasons and foreign films have never been released on Blu-ray at all. For something you'll watch once, or a show that was made in standard definition, a DVD is a perfectly sensible buy.

For anything you love and plan to watch again on a big TV, Blu-ray is the better long-term choice. And because Blu-ray players play DVDs too, there's no reason not to mix both on the same shelf.

## Start with the five DVDs you watch most

You don't have to replace a shelf to feel the difference. Pick the five DVDs you rewatch most, check that each has a good Blu-ray, and upgrade those first. Your catalog will tell you what's left, and you can decide from there one film at a time.
