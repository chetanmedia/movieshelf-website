**DVD region code 1 is the code for North America: the United States (including Puerto Rico), Canada and Bermuda.** A Region 1 DVD plays on any Region 1 player and on region-free players, but a disc locked to another region, such as Region 2 for the UK, Europe and Japan, won't play on a standard US player. Discs marked Region 0 or ALL play everywhere.

There's a second catch that region guides often skip. Most discs from outside North America are also **PAL**, a different video system from the **NTSC** used in the US. So even a region-free import can fail on an American setup. This guide covers both, plus how to check a disc before you buy it.

:::stats
- 1997 | year DVD region coding began | to stagger releases by market
- 1 | is the US DVD region | shared with Canada and Bermuda
- 8 | numbered DVD regions | plus Region 0 for "play anywhere"
:::

<div class="tldr">

**TL;DR**

- **Region 1** = US, Canada, Bermuda. **Region 2** = UK, most of Europe, Japan, the Middle East, Egypt and South Africa. **Region 4** = Latin America, Australia and New Zealand.
- **Region 0 / ALL** discs aren't region locked and play on any player.
- A US player plays Region 1 and Region 0 discs only, unless it's sold as **region-free**.
- Region 2 and 4 discs are usually **PAL**. You need a player that converts to NTSC, or a TV that accepts 50Hz.
- DVD regions have nothing to do with Blu-ray regions. Track them separately.

</div>

## What are the DVD region codes and which countries use them?

Region coding arrived with DVD in 1997 so studios could release a film at different times, and through different distributors, in each market. The player enforces it: every DVD player has a region set in its firmware, and a locked disc checks that setting before it plays. Here's the full map, based on [Wikipedia's DVD region code entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD_region_code):

| Region | Who uses it | What collectors import from it |
|---|---|---|
| **0 / ALL** | Not locked, plays worldwide | Many indie and public-domain releases |
| **1** | United States (including Puerto Rico), Canada, Bermuda | US releases, US Criterion DVDs |
| **2** | UK and most of Europe, Japan, the Middle East, Egypt, South Africa, Greenland | BFI, Arrow and Eureka DVDs, Japanese anime |
| **3** | Southeast Asia, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau | Hong Kong action and Korean films |
| **4** | Mexico, Central and South America, the Caribbean, Australia, New Zealand, most of Oceania | Australian releases |
| **5** | Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, most of Africa, Central and South Asia (including India), Mongolia, North Korea | Indian and Russian releases |
| **6** | Mainland China | Chinese releases |
| **7** | Reserved, used for some promotional and pre-release copies | Rarely seen by collectors |
| **8** | International venues such as airplanes and cruise ships | Almost never sold |

Two entries surprise people. **Japan is Region 2** on DVD, the same as the UK, which is why a Japanese DVD won't play on a US player. And **Mexico is Region 4**, not Region 1, even though it sits in the same Blu-ray region as the US.

:::note Discs can carry more than one code
A disc can be coded for several regions at once. UK and Australian releases, for example, are sometimes coded **2 and 4**. The back of the case shows every region the disc will play in.
:::

## What does a Region 1 DVD mean for you?

If you live in the US or Canada, a Region 1 DVD is the "normal" disc. It plays on every DVD player, Blu-ray player and 4K Blu-ray player sold locally, and it's in NTSC, so it works on any American TV.

It matters when you travel or move. A Region 1 disc won't play on a standard player sold in the UK, Europe, Australia or Japan. If you're relocating with a big collection, or sending discs to family abroad, check their player first.

:::cards
### Region 1 + US player
Plays. This is the standard case for anything bought in the US or Canada.
### Region 0 / ALL + US player
Plays, as long as the disc is NTSC. Many region-free discs from outside North America are PAL.
### Region 2 or 4 + US player
Region error. Your player will show a message such as "Wrong region" and refuse the disc.
### Any region + region-free player
Plays, provided the player converts PAL to NTSC or your TV accepts a 50Hz signal.
:::

## How do you check a DVD's region before you buy?

It takes ten seconds and saves a return.

:::steps
### Find the region badge on the back of the case
It's a small globe icon with a number inside, usually near the runtime and audio details. A globe with several numbers, or the word ALL, means the disc plays in each of them.
### Look for PAL or NTSC
It's usually printed in the same technical block. US discs are NTSC. UK, European and Australian discs are almost always PAL.
### Read the listing, not just the photo
Retailers and labels normally state the region in the product details. The [BFI shop](https://shop.bfi.org.uk/formats-and-region-codes/), for example, notes that its DVDs are made for Region 2 players.
### Check what your player is set to
A standalone player sold in the US is Region 1. A player that can play other regions will say "region-free" or "multi-region" in its specs.
:::

## Why won't a Region 2 DVD play in the US even on some region-free players?

Because region is only half the problem. The other half is the video system:

:::versus NTSC (Region 1) | PAL (Region 2 and 4)
- Used in the US, Canada, Mexico, Japan and South Korea
- 480 lines of resolution
- Roughly 60 fields per second (60Hz)
- Plays on every US TV
---
- Used in the UK, most of Europe, Australia, India and China
- 576 lines of resolution
- 50 fields per second (50Hz)
- Older US TVs show a scrambled or black-and-white picture
:::

A region-free player removes the region check, but it still has to send a picture your TV can display. Older American TVs couldn't show a PAL signal at all, which is why collectors used to buy players with built-in PAL-to-NTSC conversion. Many modern flat-screen TVs will display a 50Hz signal over HDMI, but not every US model does, so check your TV's specs or test with one disc before buying a stack.

:::warning Japan is the exception
Japanese DVDs are Region 2 but **NTSC**. A region-free player connected to a US TV will play them without any PAL conversion.
:::

Blu-ray players handle this better than old DVD players. Blu-ray players are backwards compatible with both PAL and NTSC DVDs, though for DVD playback they're still locked to the DVD region of the country they were sold in.

## How do DVD regions compare to Blu-ray and 4K regions?

Each disc format has its own system, and they don't line up.

:::bars Number of regions by format
- DVD | 8 | 8 numbered regions (plus 0)
- Blu-ray | 3 | Regions A, B and C
- 4K UHD Blu-ray | 0 | no region coding at all
:::

| | DVD | Blu-ray | 4K UHD Blu-ray |
|---|---|---|---|
| US code | Region 1 | Region A | None |
| UK and Europe | Region 2 | Region B | None |
| Japan | Region 2 | Region A | None |
| Mexico and Latin America | Region 4 | Region A | None |
| Video system matters? | Yes, PAL vs NTSC | Rarely | No |

So a single box can be confusing. A UK 4K combo pack is region-free on the 4K disc, but the Blu-ray inside may be locked to Region B, and an included DVD would be Region 2 and PAL. Our guide to [Blu-ray Regions A, B and C](/blog/what-blu-ray-region-is-the-us/) covers the Blu-ray side in detail, and the [4K Blu-ray player guide](/blog/uhd-4k-blu-ray-player/) explains why a 4K player still plays DVDs by region.

:::pullquote
Region coding belongs to the edition, not the film. Two copies of the same movie can play in completely different places.
:::

## Can you change the region on a DVD player?

It depends on the device:

- **Standalone US players:** Sony, for one, says there's [no way to override the region code](https://www.sony.ca/en/electronics/support/articles/00027605) on its players sold in the US. Treat a standard player as permanently Region 1.
- **Computer DVD drives:** most let you change the region up to **five times**. After the fifth change, the last region becomes permanent.
- **Region-free players:** sold as "multi-region" or "region-free", these ignore the region check. Look for PAL-to-NTSC conversion in the specs if you plan to watch European or Australian discs on a US TV.

If you buy imports regularly, a dedicated region-free player is the least fiddly option. It's also worth keeping a short list of which discs actually need it, so you know before movie night.

## How should you track regions in your collection?

Once you own even a handful of imports, you'll want to know which discs play where, especially if you lend discs out, travel or keep a second player in another room. Record it per edition, the same way you'd record the format:

:::checklist Region details worth recording
- **DVD region** (1, 2, 4, 0 and so on) for every disc that isn't Region 1
- **PAL or NTSC**, for anything from Europe, the UK or Australia
- **Blu-ray region** (A, B, C or free) for any Blu-ray in the same box
- **Which player** plays it, if you own more than one
- **Where you bought it**, since import retailers vary in how they label regions
:::

## Check the back of the case before you buy

Region codes sound complicated, but for a US collector they boil down to one habit: look for the globe badge and the word PAL before you buy an import. If it says 1, 0 or ALL and NTSC, it plays. Anything else needs a region-free player, and is worth noting in your catalog the day it arrives.
