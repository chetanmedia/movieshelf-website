**Disc rot is the slow chemical breakdown of the reflective layer inside a DVD, Blu-ray or CD, usually from oxidation or failing adhesive, until the laser can't read the data.** It shows up as pinholes, cloudy or bronze stains, or discs that freeze at the same spot every time. It can't be repaired, but most of it is preventable with cool, dry, upright storage, and you can check a whole shelf for it in an afternoon.

:::stats
- 2006–2008 | the Warner Bros. DVDs with confirmed rot problems | Warner offered replacements in 2025
- 20–100 years | estimated life of pressed discs | NIST care guide
- 18°C / 40% RH | NIST benchmark for long-term disc storage | about 64°F
:::

## What is disc rot, exactly?

Every pressed disc is a plastic sandwich. The data lives as microscopic pits in a very thin metal reflective layer, and the player reads it by bouncing a laser off that layer. Disc rot is anything that damages the layer from the inside. As [Wikipedia's disc rot entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_rot) summarizes, the main culprits are oxidation of the reflective layer, reactions with contaminants, UV exposure, and the adhesive between layers coming unstuck.

Where that metal sits depends on the format, and that's why each format fails a little differently:

| Format | Reflective layer | Where it sits | Main rot risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| CD | Aluminum | Just under the thin lacquer on the label side | Scratches or solvents on the label side expose the metal |
| DVD | Aluminum | Bonded between two 0.6 mm plastic halves | Bad adhesive or contaminated bonding lets air and moisture in |
| Blu-ray | Usually a silver alloy | Under a thin 0.1 mm protective cover layer | Rare, but reported on some dual-layer BD-50s |
| 4K UHD | Silver alloy, two or three layers | Same Blu-ray construction | Too new for meaningful long-term data |

So a DVD is actually better protected than a CD, because the metal is sealed in the middle of the disc. When DVDs do rot, it is usually because something went wrong when the two halves were glued together at the factory.

:::pullquote
Disc rot is rarely something you did. It is usually something the factory did, and storage decides how fast it shows.
:::

## What does disc rot look like?

Rot doesn't look like a scratch. Scratches are on the surface and follow the direction of a wipe or a slide. Rot is *inside* the disc and looks like a stain or a speckle that you can't touch.

:::cards
### Pinholes
Tiny clear dots that let light through when you hold the disc up to a bright lamp. The metal has corroded away at those points.
### Cloudy or coffee stains
Milky patches or brownish blotches inside the disc, often spreading in from the edge.
### Bronzing
A bronze or copper tint across part of the disc, a classic sign of oxidized aluminum.
### Edge delamination
The layers starting to separate at the outer rim or around the center hole. You may see a ring or a bubble.
### Invisible rot
The disc looks perfect but freezes, pixelates or refuses to load. Many collectors only find rot this way.
:::

The playback symptoms matter as much as the visual ones. A rotting disc typically fails at the **same spot every time**, often later in the film, because discs are read from the center outward and rot often starts at the edge. Dirt and light scratches, by contrast, tend to cause random skips that a cleaning fixes.

:::versus Scratch or dirt | Disc rot
- On the surface; you can feel or wipe it
- Follows a line or smudge
- Often fixed by cleaning
- Any disc, anytime
---
- Inside the disc; the surface is smooth
- Pinholes, stains, bronzing or nothing visible
- Never fixed by cleaning or polishing
- Clusters by title, year and pressing plant
:::

## What causes disc rot?

There are two buckets, and the first one matters far more.

**Manufacturing defects.** Contaminated or poorly cured adhesive, an aluminum layer that is too thin, or a bad batch of materials. This is why rot clusters in specific releases instead of being spread evenly across everyone's shelf. The best-known example is Warner Bros.: in 2025, Warner acknowledged that some DVDs it manufactured between 2006 and 2008 were rotting and began replacing them. As [The A.V. Club reported](https://www.avclub.com/warner-bros-replacing-disc-rot-dvd), the catch is that out-of-print titles, or titles whose rights have expired, may be swapped for a different title of similar value rather than the same film.

**Storage conditions.** Heat, humidity, pollutants and sunlight all speed up chemical reactions in a weak disc. A well-made disc can shrug off a warm room for decades. A disc from a bad batch will rot faster in a hot attic than in a cool closet.

:::note Blu-ray and 4K owners
Blu-rays are not immune. Collectors have reported failing Blu-rays from several studios, many of them dual-layer BD-50 discs, but there has been no confirmed, studio-acknowledged batch on the scale of the Warner DVDs. 4K UHD discs haven't been on the market long enough for anyone to know their long-term failure rate.
:::

## How do you check your collection for disc rot?

You don't need special equipment. Do one shelf at a time, and start with the riskiest discs.

:::steps
### Pull the high-risk discs first
Warner Bros. DVDs from 2006–2008, anything stored in a garage, attic or car, and discs bought used with unknown history.
### Do the light test
In a dim room, hold each disc up to a bright lamp or a phone flashlight, data side toward you. Look for pinpricks of light, cloudy patches and bronze tints.
### Check the edges and hub
Tilt the disc and look along the outer rim and the clear ring near the center for bubbles, rings or separating layers.
### Clean before you judge
Wipe in straight lines from the center to the edge with a soft cotton or microfiber cloth. Never wipe in circles.
### Play the end, not the start
Skip to the last chapters and the menus. Rot often shows up near the outer edge, which is where the end of the film lives.
### Record the result
Note which discs passed, which are suspect and which have failed, so you never have to retest the same shelf from memory.
:::

## Can disc rot be fixed?

No. The damage is inside the disc, under the plastic, so there is nothing for a cleaner, polish or resurfacing machine to reach. Resurfacing removes scratches from the *outer* plastic; it can't restore metal that has corroded away. What you can do:

- **Rule out dirt and scratches first.** A proper cleaning fixes more "rotted" discs than you'd expect.
- **Try a different player.** Some players handle marginal discs better than others. This can get you through one more viewing, but it won't stop the decline.
- **Ask for a replacement.** If the title is a 2006–2008 Warner DVD, contact Warner Bros. Home Entertainment's customer support about its exchange.
- **Rebuy, ideally a newer pressing.** A later reissue or the Blu-ray or 4K upgrade is often cheaper than you'd think, and comes from a different production run.

:::warning Don't toss it yet
If you're filing a replacement request, keep the failed disc and its case until the claim is settled. You may be asked to show what you're replacing.
:::

## How do you prevent disc rot?

You can't fix a bad pressing, but you can stop good discs from going bad and slow down weak ones. The [NIST guide for librarians and archivists](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/legacy/sp/NISTspecialpublication500-252.pdf) recommends storing discs below 20°C (68°F) at 20–50% relative humidity, and calls 18°C and 40% RH suitable for long-term storage. For a home collection, that translates to a few simple habits:

:::checklist Disc rot prevention routine
- Keep discs in a **cool, dry, dark room**: not the attic, garage, basement floor or a sunny window wall.
- Avoid **temperature swings**. If discs come in from a cold car or storage unit, let them warm up slowly in their cases.
- Store discs **upright, like books**, in proper DVD or Blu-ray cases.
- Hold discs **by the edge and the center hole**, never the data surface.
- Wipe **center to edge** in straight lines, never in circles.
- Don't put **adhesive labels** or solvent-based markers on discs.
- **Spot-check** your oldest and highest-risk discs every year or so.
:::

Upright storage also makes your shelf easier to browse. Our guide to [organizing a DVD collection](/blog/how-to-organize-dvd-collection/) covers shelving systems that work with vertical storage, and the [Organize section](/#organize) shows how shelf locations work in the app.

## Which discs are most at risk?

Not every disc deserves the same worry. Roughly, from highest to lowest risk:

| Risk | Discs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| High | Warner Bros. DVDs made 2006–2008 | Acknowledged manufacturing problem |
| High | Any disc stored hot, damp or in sunlight | Conditions accelerate weak discs |
| Medium | Older dual-layer discs, used discs with unknown history | Bonding issues; you don't know where they've been |
| Medium | Burned DVD-Rs and CD-Rs | Dye layers degrade faster than pressed metal |
| Lower | Recent Blu-ray and 4K UHD discs stored well | Better-protected layers and less time to age |
