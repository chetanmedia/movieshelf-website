**To clean a DVD, Blu-ray or CD, blow off loose dust first, then wipe the shiny side in straight lines from the center hole out to the edge with a soft, lint-free cloth.** Use distilled water for light marks and a drop of mild dish soap or isopropyl alcohol for fingerprints and grease, applied to the cloth, not the disc. Never wipe in circles, and never use paper towels, glass cleaner or anything abrasive.

That's the whole method, and it's what archives use too. The rest of this guide covers the details that trip collectors up: why the direction matters, why CDs need gentler handling than DVDs, what's different about Blu-ray and 4K discs, and how to tell when a disc needs more than a wipe.

1. **Dust first:** a blower or canned air, so grit isn't dragged across the disc.

6. Still skipping after a clean wipe? It's a **scratch or a damaged layer**, not dirt.

## What do you need to clean DVDs and CDs?

Nothing special. Everything on this list is probably already in your house, and a dedicated disc-cleaning kit is optional rather than essential.

:::cards
### A soft, lint-free cloth
Clean cotton or a fresh microfiber cloth like the ones sold for glasses. Wash it without fabric softener, which leaves a film.
### Distilled water
Tap water works in a pinch, but distilled water dries without mineral spots.
### Mild dish soap
One or two drops in a bowl of lukewarm water cuts through greasy fingerprints.
### Isopropyl alcohol
For stubborn marks and sticky residue. The [NIST disc care guide](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080916073248/http:/www.itl.nist.gov/iad/894.05/docs/onepage.pdf) lists it alongside disc-cleaning detergent as safe.
### A blower or canned air
Lifts dust before you touch the disc. Archive care guides, including the Library of Congress, recommend canned air for dust.
:::

## How do you clean a DVD step by step?

This works for DVDs, Blu-rays, 4K UHD discs, CDs and game discs alike. Start with the gentlest step and stop as soon as the disc is clean.

:::steps
### Blow off loose dust
Use a rubber blower or short bursts of canned air held upright, so no propellant sprays out. Grit is what scratches discs during cleaning, so get it off before anything touches the surface.
### Hold the disc by its edge and center hole
Rest a finger in the hole and a thumb on the rim. Fingerprints on the shiny side are oil, and oil attracts more dust.
### Dampen the cloth, not the disc
A light mist of distilled water on the cloth is enough for most marks. Liquid sprayed on the disc runs to the edge and center hole.
### Wipe in straight lines from the center out
Short, light strokes from the hub to the rim, working around the disc like the spokes of a wheel. Turn the cloth to a clean section often.
### Step up only if needed
For fingerprints and grease, use a drop of dish soap in lukewarm water, or a little isopropyl alcohol on the cloth. Same direction, same light pressure.
### Dry it and let it air-dry
Pat dry with a dry part of the cloth, then leave the disc label-side down on a clean towel for a few minutes before you play it or put it away.
:::

## Why should you never wipe a disc in circles?

This is the one rule every care guide agrees on, including the [National Archives condition guide](https://www.archives.gov/files/preservation/formats/pdf/optical-discs-condition-assessment-table.pdf) and NIST. The reason is the way data is laid out.

A disc stores its data in one long spiral track running from the center out. A circular wipe moves along that track, so any grit caught in the cloth can leave a scratch that follows the spiral and damages a long continuous stretch of data. A straight stroke from center to edge crosses the track at right angles, so a scratch only touches a tiny bit of each loop. The player's error correction can usually fill in those small gaps.

:::versus Center to edge | In circles
- Scratches cross the track briefly
- Error correction covers the small gaps
- Recommended by NIST and the National Archives
- Easy to see which areas you've done
---
- Scratches run along the data track
- Damage hits a long, continuous stretch
- The classic cause of a disc that skips in one spot
- Pushes grit around instead of off the edge
:::

:::pullquote
Clean the way the data isn't: straight across the track, never along it.
:::

## Are CDs, DVDs and Blu-rays cleaned the same way?

The method is the same. What changes is how much protection sits between your cloth and the data, and that changes where you need to be careful.

:::bars How deep the data sits below the shiny read side
- CD | 1.2 | ~1.2 mm
- DVD | 0.6 | ~0.6 mm
- Blu-ray and 4K UHD | 0.1 | ~0.1 mm
:::

That chart has a catch for CDs. The data sits deep below the shiny side, but only a thin lacquer and the label sit above it on top. That's why the National Archives guide lists scratches on top of a CD as a cause of readability problems, while scratches on top of a DVD usually leave it playable.

| Disc type | Fragile side | What it means when cleaning |
|---|---|---|
| CD | Label side (top) | Never scrub or scrape the label, and don't write on it with a ballpoint pen. The read side forgives more. |
| DVD | Neither, especially | Data sits in the middle of two bonded halves, so scratches on either side are roughly equivalent. |
| Blu-ray / 4K UHD | Read side, in theory | The data is only about 0.1 mm down, but a hard coating protects it. Clean gently and skip anything abrasive. |

Samsung's [disc format comparison](https://www.samsung.com/in/support/tv-audio-video/what-is-the-difference-between-blu-ray-disc-dvd-and-cd/) lists the 0.6 mm DVD and 0.1 mm Blu-ray protection layers, and notes the hard coating on Blu-ray. That coating is why Blu-rays and 4K discs often survive handling that would mark a DVD, but it's also a reason not to attack them with polish or harsh household cleaners.

:::note Rewritable and homemade discs
Burned DVD-Rs and CD-Rs use a dye layer instead of pressed pits, and the label side of a CD-R is just as fragile as a pressed CD. Clean them the same way, but go even lighter, and keep alcohol away from any printed or handwritten label.
:::

## What should you never use to clean a disc?

Most disc damage people blame on age is actually done during cleaning. Every item here turns up in "life hack" videos, and every one is a bad idea for a collection you care about.

:::cards
### Paper towels and tissues
Wood fibers are abrasive and leave lint. NARA's guide suggests soft cotton or chamois instead.
### Your T-shirt
It carries the dust you're trying to remove, and you'll press harder than you think.
### Glass cleaner
Many contain ammonia or other chemicals that aren't made for polycarbonate or Blu-ray coatings.
### Acetone and strong solvents
Nail polish remover, paint thinner and similar solvents can cloud or dissolve the plastic.
### Toothpaste and polishes
They're abrasives. They belong to scratch repair, not cleaning, and they remove plastic every time.
### Circular rubbing and hard pressure
Pressure grinds any remaining grit into the surface. Light strokes, clean cloth, repeat.
:::

## How do you clean used or thrifted DVDs?

Discs from thrift stores, flea markets and library sales usually need more than a quick wipe, but they're also where cleaning pays off most. A dirty disc that "doesn't play" is often a perfectly good disc.

1. **Check before you clean.** Hold the disc up to a light. Grime, smudges and a hazy film are cleaning jobs. Deep scratches, pinholes of light or cloudy, bronze or speckled patches in the layer are not.
2. **Rinse off grit.** A quick rinse under lukewarm running water floats away sand and dust that a dry cloth would drag across the surface.
3. **Wash greasy discs.** A drop of dish soap in lukewarm water, a center-to-edge wipe, then a rinse and a careful dry.
4. **Lift sticker residue with isopropyl alcohol** on a cloth or cotton swab, working gently. Don't scrape it with a fingernail or blade.
5. **Clean the case too.** Dust and grime in a case end up straight back on the disc. Wipe the inside, and replace cracked hubs; our [case replacement guide](/blog/dvd-case-replacement/) has the sizes.
6. **Test play the whole disc**, or at least skip through each chapter, before deciding it's a keeper or a return.

:::warning Cleaning can't fix disc rot
If you see discoloration, speckles or patches that shine through when you hold the disc to a light, the problem is inside the disc, not on it. Cleaning won't help. Our guide to [disc rot](/blog/disc-rot/) shows what it looks like and what to do next.
:::

## What if the disc still skips after cleaning?

Clean it once, test it, and if it still stalls at the same point, the cause is almost certainly physical damage rather than dirt. At that point you have a few options, roughly in order of risk:

- **Try another player.** Drives differ in how well they read marginal discs. If it plays elsewhere, the disc is still readable.
- **Price a replacement.** Many popular films are cheap to replace, often in a better format than the one you have.
- **Consider resurfacing.** Professional resurfacing machines and DIY polishing remove a thin layer of plastic to smooth out scratches. It's a last resort, best kept for discs you can't easily replace, and it's a different job from cleaning.

Whatever you decide, write it down. A disc that skips in chapter 12 is easy to forget until movie night.

## How do you keep discs clean in the first place?

The best cleaning routine is the one you rarely need. A few habits keep most discs clean for years:

:::checklist Keep your discs clean
- Handle discs **only by the edge and center hole**.
- Put every disc **straight back in its case** after watching, never on a table.
- Keep cases **closed and upright** on the shelf.
- Dust shelves regularly so dust doesn't settle into open or worn cases.
- Keep food and drinks away from the player during movie night.
- Replace cases with **broken hubs**, which let discs rattle and rub.
- Store discs in a **cool, dry room** away from attics, garages and sunny walls.
:::

Storage does most of the work here. Our guide to [how to store DVDs](/blog/how-to-store-dvds/) covers cases, sleeves and room conditions in detail.

## Clean one disc tonight

Pick the disc that froze the last time you watched it, give it a center-to-edge wipe with a soft cloth and a little water, and try it again. More often than not, that's all it needed.
