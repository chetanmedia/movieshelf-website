The easiest way to track which shelf every movie is on is to **give each shelf a short address, label it, and record that address on every disc in your catalog**. An address like *Living room · B3* (unit B, shelf 3) is precise enough to walk straight to, and alphabetical order within the shelf handles the last few inches. Skip slot numbers: they break the moment you add a new disc in the middle.

Most guides to organizing a collection stop at "alphabetize it." That works until the collection spills into a second room, a closet of boxes or a binder, or until someone else in the house goes looking for *Heat*. This guide is the location layer on top of whatever shelving order you already use.

:::stats
- 3 | parts to a good shelf address | room · unit · shelf
- 1 | field to change when a disc moves | its location
- 0 | slot numbers to renumber | when a new disc arrives
:::

<div class="tldr">

**TL;DR**

1. Address shelves, not slots: *room · unit · shelf*, written as *Living room · B3*.
2. Label each shelf edge with its code so anyone can read the system.
3. Record the address on every title in your catalog as you scan.
4. Number boxes and binders the same way, because you can't read their spines.
5. Use temporary locations like *To shelve* and *Lent to Sam* instead of leaving one wrong.

</div>

## Why does "it's somewhere on the shelves" stop working?

Alphabetical order gets you surprisingly far, as long as all your discs live on one wall. Collectors on long-running forum threads at DVD Talk and High-Def Digest describe most of the points where it breaks:

:::cards
### Overflow storage
Boxes in a closet, sleeves in a binder or a trunk of discs. You can't read a spine through a lid, so without a location note those discs are effectively lost.
### Split sections
Box sets on the bottom shelf, steelbooks on a display shelf, Criterion by spine number. Each zone has its own order, so "alphabetical" no longer tells you where to look.
### The new-arrival pile
Discs that haven't been shelved yet pile up in a stack, and that's exactly where the film you want is hiding.
### Other people
A partner, a roommate or a house-sitter can't read your mental map. A label and a catalog entry can.
:::

The fix is the same idea libraries use. A library's call number points to a place on the shelves, not just a subject, which is why you can find one book among hundreds of thousands. (Boston Public Library has a [plain-English guide to how call numbers work](https://bpl.org/blogs/post/whats-with-all-the-letters-and-numbers-a-very-basic-guide-to-our-call-numbers) if you're curious.) You don't need anything that elaborate. You need a short code for each shelf.

## What should a shelf address look like?

A shelf address has three parts: the **room**, the **unit** (a bookcase, tower, cabinet or box) and the **shelf** within it. Keep codes short enough to fit on a small label and to type on a phone.

| Address level | Example | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Room | Living room, Office, Hall closet | Your collection lives in more than one room |
| Unit | A, B, Tower 2, Box 4, Binder 1 | A room has more than one bookcase, tower or box |
| Shelf | 1 (top) to 6 (bottom) | Always; it's the level you actually walk to |
| Slot | 14th from the left | Rarely; only for fixed-order lines like Criterion spine numbers |

Put together, that gives you addresses like these:

- *Living room · A2* for unit A, shelf 2
- *Office · Tower 1 · 5* for the fifth shelf of a CD-style tower
- *Hall closet · Box 4* for a storage box
- *Bedroom · Binder 2 · Page 18* for sleeved discs

:::tip Number shelves top to bottom
Count shelves from the top, starting at 1. Everyone reads a bookcase that way, so "B3" means the same thing to you, your partner and a friend helping you move.
:::

## Should you number every slot on the shelf?

This is where most location systems quietly fail. Slot numbers feel precise, but on an alphabetical shelf every new disc shifts everything to its right. Buy *Arrival* on 4K and slots 2 through 22 are all wrong in your catalog.

:::versus Address the shelf | Number every slot
- New discs slot in without touching the catalog
- A shelf holds roughly 20 to 30 cases, easy to scan by eye
- Works with any shelving order
- One edit when a disc moves
---
- Every insertion renumbers the rest of the shelf
- Catalog goes stale within weeks
- Only stable for fixed-order lines, like Criterion spine numbers
- Tempting, precise and almost never kept up
:::

Shelf-level addressing works because a shelf is a small search space. At roughly 14 mm per standard DVD case and 11 to 13 mm per Blu-ray or 4K case (see our [DVD case replacement guide](/blog/dvd-case-replacement/) for exact sizes), a one-foot run holds a couple of dozen discs, which you can scan in seconds.

:::bars Roughly how many cases fit in one foot (305 mm) of shelf
- Standard DVD (14 mm) | 21 | about 21
- Blu-ray or 4K (11–13 mm) | 25 | about 23–27
- Slim DVD (7–8 mm) | 40 | about 38–43
:::

The exception is a numbered line. If you shelve [Criterion Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Criterion_Collection) releases by spine number, the spine number *is* the slot address, and it never shifts because Criterion assigns it, not you.

## How do you set up shelf addresses in one afternoon?

You don't need to reorganize anything. The address layer goes on top of the shelves you already have.

:::steps
### Sketch the layout
Walk each room and list every bookcase, tower, cabinet and box. Give each one a letter or number, left to right as you face it.
### Label the shelf edges
Put a small label on the front edge of every shelf: *A1*, *A2*, *B1* and so on. Removable label-maker tape or small round stickers work and won't damage wood.
### Number your boxes and binders
Write the code on two sides of every box, so it's readable however it's stacked. Number binder pages if you store discs in sleeves.
### Record the address as you catalog
Scan or add a shelf's worth of discs, then give them all the same location. Doing it one shelf at a time keeps the job short and the data consistent.
### Add the temporary locations
Create *To shelve* for new arrivals and *Lent to …* for loans, so a disc's location is never simply wrong.
:::

If you haven't cataloged your collection yet, do both jobs in one pass. Our step-by-step guide to [cataloging a DVD and Blu-ray collection](/blog/how-to-catalog-dvd-blu-ray-collection/) covers scanning in batches; adding a location while each shelf is in front of you costs a few extra seconds.

## How do you track discs that aren't on a shelf?

Boxes, binders and loans are where a location system pays for itself, because you can't browse them.

### Storage boxes
Treat each box as a unit with one shelf: *Hall closet · Box 4*. Keep the box contents roughly alphabetical too, so finding a disc inside takes seconds once you've opened the right one.

### Binders and sleeves
Address binders down to the page: *Binder 2 · Page 18*. This is the one case where the finest level is worth recording, because binders don't get new pages inserted in the middle as often as shelves get new discs.

### Discs you've lent out
Change the location to the borrower's name, like *Lent to Sam*. It's the same field, so searching for the film tells you it's out of the house and who has it.

### The new-arrival pile
Give it a real location, *To shelve*, and let new discs sit there in your catalog until they find a home. Collectors on the DVD Talk forums describe exactly this pile of unshelved discs; recording it means it never becomes a black hole.

## How do you keep locations accurate when things move?

A location system is only as good as its last update. The good news is that shelf-level addresses keep updates small.

- **Move whole shelves, not single discs.** If shelf B3 moves to the office, update one shelf's worth of entries to *Office · B3* in a sitting. The labels travel with the shelf, so only the room part changes.
- **Leave slack on every shelf.** An inch or two of empty space at the end means new discs slide in without pushing the last few onto the next shelf.
- **Re-label, don't re-number.** If a unit fills up, add a new unit letter (C, D) rather than renumbering A and B.
- **Fix on sight.** If search sends you to the wrong shelf, correct that entry before you put the disc back.

:::warning The most common way catalogs drift
Taking a disc out to watch and putting it back on a different shelf. It's harmless once, and after a year it's why search sends you to the wrong place. Put discs back where the label says, or update the location there and then.
:::

:::checklist Keep your shelf addresses accurate
- Every shelf, box and binder has a visible label
- New discs go to *To shelve* the day they arrive
- Loans are recorded as *Lent to …*, and changed back on return
- Every shelf has an inch or two of slack
- One shelf audited against the catalog every few months
:::

## Does shelving order still matter once you track locations?

Less than you'd think. Once search tells you the shelf, the order on that shelf only has to make a couple of dozen discs easy to scan. That frees you to shelve the way you enjoy: by genre, by director, by label or by what looks good on display. Our comparison of [nine ways to organize a DVD collection](/blog/how-to-organize-dvd-collection/) covers the trade-offs of each.

:::pullquote
Once every shelf has an address, the order on the shelf becomes a matter of taste, not a filing system.
:::

The one order we'd still recommend inside each shelf is alphabetical, because it's the fastest way to do that last step by eye. If you want to show off by genre or color, do it on a display shelf and keep the address on it like any other.
