**HDR10 and Dolby Vision are both high dynamic range formats on 4K Blu-ray, and the difference is metadata: HDR10 gives your TV one set of brightness instructions for the whole film, while Dolby Vision adjusts them scene by scene.** Every Ultra HD Blu-ray carries HDR10, and some add a Dolby Vision layer on top. To see Dolby Vision, your player, your TV and anything in between must all support it; otherwise the disc plays in HDR10 automatically.

So you never "lose" a movie by picking the wrong format. The real questions are whether your gear can use Dolby Vision, and whether it makes a visible difference on your TV. This guide answers both, and shows how to keep track of which discs on your shelf actually have it.

:::stats
- 10-bit | HDR10 color, on every 4K Blu-ray | about 1.07 billion shades
- 12-bit | Dolby Vision's maximum color depth | about 68.7 billion shades
- 10,000 | nits, the ceiling of the PQ curve both formats use | real discs are mastered far lower
:::

<div class="tldr">

**TL;DR**

- **HDR10** is the mandatory baseline on every 4K Blu-ray. Static metadata: one setting for the whole film.
- **Dolby Vision** is optional. Dynamic metadata: the TV gets fresh instructions for each scene or frame.
- Dolby Vision discs **fall back to HDR10** on players or TVs that don't support it.
- The gain is biggest on **mid-range TVs** that can't hit the film's mastering brightness. On a very bright OLED or mini-LED it can be subtle.
- Samsung TVs and the PS5 and Xbox Series X **can't** show Dolby Vision from disc.

</div>

## What is the difference between HDR10 and Dolby Vision?

Both formats use the same foundation: 3840×2160 resolution, a wide color gamut (Rec. 2020) and the PQ brightness curve standardized as SMPTE ST 2084. The picture data on the disc is the same kind of signal. What differs is the extra information that tells your TV how to display it.

:::versus HDR10 | Dolby Vision
- Static metadata: one set of values for the entire film
- 10-bit color
- Open standard, no license fee
- On every 4K Blu-ray
- Works on every HDR TV and UHD player
---
- Dynamic metadata, scene by scene or frame by frame
- Up to 12-bit color
- Licensed from Dolby
- On some 4K Blu-rays only
- Needs a Dolby Vision player, TV and HDMI chain
:::

HDR10's static metadata describes the brightest pixel in the whole film and its average brightness level. Your TV uses those two numbers to decide how to squeeze the picture into what its panel can do. That works well when the TV is bright enough to show the film as mastered. It works less well when a film has a blazing desert scene and a dim interior, because one compromise setting has to serve both.

Dolby Vision sends new instructions as the film plays, so the TV can treat the dark cellar and the sunlit exterior differently. Dolby explains the idea in its own [HDR10 vs Dolby Vision overview](https://www.dolby.com/experience/home-entertainment/articles/the-difference-between-hdr10-and-dolby-vision/).

:::pullquote
HDR10 tells your TV how to show the whole film. Dolby Vision tells it how to show this scene.
:::

## Why does tone mapping matter so much?

Films are mastered on reference monitors, commonly at 1,000 or 4,000 nits peak. Most home TVs can't reach 4,000 nits, and many mid-range sets struggle to hold 1,000 across a large bright area. The TV has to "tone map": compress highlights it can't reproduce without crushing detail or dimming the whole image.

:::bars Peak brightness, in nits
- SDR reference (standard Blu-ray) | 100 | 100
- Common HDR master | 1000 | 1,000
- High HDR master | 4000 | 4,000
- PQ curve ceiling | 10000 | 10,000
:::

This is where dynamic metadata earns its keep. With only a whole-film maximum, a TV may dim every scene to protect the brightest one. With per-scene data, it can leave a dark scene alone and only compress the shots that actually exceed its limits. The result, on the right TV, is better shadow detail and highlights that don't clip.

:::note Dynamic doesn't mean brighter
Dolby Vision isn't a "brighter" or "more colorful" mode. It's more accurate guidance. If a disc looks dimmer in Dolby Vision than in HDR10, that's often the TV following the creator's intent instead of boosting the image.
:::

## How is Dolby Vision stored on a 4K Blu-ray?

Ultra HD Blu-ray uses a dual-layer approach (Dolby Vision Profile 7). The disc holds a standard HDR10 video stream, the **base layer**, plus a Dolby Vision **enhancement layer** that carries the dynamic metadata. That design is why the same disc plays everywhere.

:::cards
### HDR10 base layer
A complete 10-bit HDR10 picture. Every UHD player can read it, so every disc plays.
### Enhancement layer
The Dolby Vision extra. Players and TVs that support Dolby Vision combine it with the base layer.
### MEL (minimum enhancement layer)
Carries the Dolby Vision metadata only, with no extra picture data. Many Dolby Vision discs use it.
### FEL (full enhancement layer)
Adds residual picture data that can take the image to 12-bit. Enthusiasts prize FEL discs, though the visible gain is debated.
:::

If you rip discs to a media server, this matters: many streaming boxes and players handle the HDR10 base layer and MEL well but can't fully decode FEL. For plain disc playback, a Dolby Vision player handles all of it.

## What do you need to watch Dolby Vision 4K Blu-rays?

Every link in the chain has to support Dolby Vision from physical discs. Miss one, and you get HDR10.

:::steps
### A player with Dolby Vision disc playback
The Panasonic DP-UB820 and up do it. Some cheaper Panasonic models and both disc consoles (PS5, Xbox Series X) play UHD discs in HDR10 only. Our [UHD 4K Blu-ray player guide](/blog/uhd-4k-blu-ray-player/) lists which models support what.
### A TV with Dolby Vision
Check the spec sheet, not the box art. LG, Sony, Panasonic, TCL, Hisense and Philips sell Dolby Vision TVs. Samsung TVs support HDR10 and HDR10+, not Dolby Vision.
### An HDMI chain that passes it through
If the player feeds a receiver or soundbar first, that device must pass Dolby Vision too. Many collectors send video straight to the TV and audio to the receiver from a second HDMI output.
### Player output set to Auto
Leave HDR output on automatic. Forcing Dolby Vision on HDR10 discs can shift brightness and color in ways the original master never intended.
:::

:::warning Streaming support isn't disc support
A TV box or console that shows Dolby Vision on Netflix may still play 4K discs in HDR10. Check for "Dolby Vision" under Ultra HD Blu-ray playback specifically.
:::

## Is Dolby Vision worth it on 4K Blu-ray?

It depends more on your TV than on the disc. Here's an honest breakdown:

| Your setup | Is Dolby Vision worth chasing? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Samsung TV | No | The TV can't display it, so you'll see HDR10 (or HDR10+ on the few discs that have it) |
| Budget or mid-range LCD | Yes, often | Lower peak brightness means more tone mapping, where per-scene data helps most |
| Bright OLED or mini-LED | Sometimes | Good HDR10 tone mapping narrows the gap; differences show in mixed bright/dark scenes |
| Projector | Rarely | Most home projectors don't support Dolby Vision and rely on their own tone mapping |
| PS5 or Xbox as your player | Not yet | Consoles play discs in HDR10 only; you'd need a new player first |

The transfer matters more than the badge. A careful HDR10 grade from a fresh 4K scan will look better than a Dolby Vision layer added to an old master. ## Where does HDR10+ fit in?

HDR10+ is the third format you'll see on some cases. Like Dolby Vision, it uses dynamic metadata. Unlike Dolby Vision, it's royalty-free and was developed with Samsung's backing, which is why Samsung TVs use it instead.

On disc, HDR10+ is much less common than Dolby Vision, and a handful of releases carry both, such as the [Back to the Future trilogy on 4K](/blog/back-to-the-future-4k-vs-blu-ray/), which has Dolby Vision, HDR10+ and HDR10 on the same discs. Like Dolby Vision, it sits on top of HDR10, so an HDR10+ disc plays normally on any UHD player and TV. If you own a Samsung TV and a player that supports HDR10+, such as Panasonic's DP-UB820, you get the dynamic benefit on the discs that have it.

:::tip How to tell which format a disc has
Check the back of the case for the Dolby Vision or HDR10+ logo, usually in the tech specs strip near the bottom. If there's no extra logo, assume HDR10 only. Your player's info display will also show which format is actually being output.
:::
